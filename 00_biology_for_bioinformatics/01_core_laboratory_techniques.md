## Practical 1 — Microscopy

Microscopy is the use of a microscope to observe biological samples or structures that cannot be seen clearly with the naked eye. It is important in biotechnology, cell biology, microbiology, and cell culture.

### Compound Microscope

A compound microscope is an optical microscope that uses multiple lenses to produce a magnified image of small biological specimens.

Its two main lens systems are:

1. Eyepiece or ocular lens
2. Objective lens

**Main parts**

- Eyepiece
- Objective lenses
- Revolving nosepiece
- Stage and stage clips
- Condenser
- Iris diaphragm
- Light source
- Coarse and fine adjustment knobs
- Arm and base

| Objective lens | Main use |
|---|---|
| 4× | Locating the sample |
| 10× | General observation |
| 40× | Observing cellular details |
| 100× | Bacteria and very small structures; oil immersion |

### Objective Lens and Total Magnification

The objective lens is positioned close to the specimen and is one of the most important magnifying lenses.

**Total magnification = Objective magnification × Eyepiece magnification**

Example:

- Eyepiece = 10×
- Objective = 40×
- Total magnification = 40 × 10 = **400×**

### Eyepiece, Magnification, and Resolution

The eyepiece is the lens through which the specimen is viewed. A common eyepiece provides 10× magnification. The objective captures optical information from the specimen, while the eyepiece further magnifies the image.

**Magnification** describes how much larger an object appears than its actual size.

**Resolution** is the ability to distinguish two closely spaced objects or structures as separate entities.

- Magnification = how large the object appears
- Resolution = how clearly details can be distinguished

More magnification does not necessarily mean more detail. If resolution is insufficient, increasing magnification may produce a larger but blurry image. This is called **empty magnification**.

### Slide Preparation

**Sample → Slide → Mounting → Coverslip → Observation**

1. Take a clean slide.
2. Place the sample on the slide.
3. Apply a stain if necessary.
4. Place a coverslip.
5. Remove excess liquid.
6. Observe under the microscope.

A coverslip holds the specimen in place, reduces drying, protects the sample, and provides a suitable optical surface for observation.

### Staining

Many biological samples are naturally transparent. Staining increases contrast between cellular structures and the background, making details easier to visualize.

| Stain | Typical use |
|---|---|
| Gram stain | Bacterial classification into Gram-positive and Gram-negative groups |
| Methylene blue | Visualization of some cells and nuclear structures |
| Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) | Histology |

The condenser focuses and directs light onto the specimen to improve illumination and image quality. Coarse adjustment is used for larger focusing movements, while fine adjustment is used for precise focusing. Oil immersion uses immersion oil with a high-power objective, typically 100×, to improve numerical aperture and resolution by reducing light refraction.

## Practical 2 — Cell Counting

Cell counting is essential in cell culture because accurate experiments require a known number of cells per well or vessel. It establishes cell concentration, supports correct dilution, and enables reliable seeding.

### Hemocytometer and Cell Concentration

A hemocytometer is a specialized counting chamber used to count cells in a defined volume of liquid and determine cell concentration. The calculation uses the chamber grid area, chamber depth, and number of cells counted.

Cell concentration is commonly expressed as **cells/mL**.

Example: 2 × 10⁶ cells in 1 mL corresponds to **2 × 10⁶ cells/mL**.

### Trypan Blue Exclusion

Trypan blue exclusion distinguishes viable from dead or membrane-compromised cells. Viable cells generally have intact membranes and exclude the dye, whereas compromised cells take up the dye and appear blue.

| Cell type | Appearance with Trypan blue |
|---|---|
| Viable cells | Generally unstained |
| Dead or compromised cells | Blue |

### Cell Viability

**Cell viability (%) = Viable cells / Total cells × 100**

Example: 85 viable cells out of 100 total cells gives **85% viability**.

### Why Cell Counting Matters

- Cell seeding
- Cell culture
- Drug treatment
- Cytotoxicity assays
- Transfection
- Experimental reproducibility
- Growth studies

**Cell counting → Cell concentration → Dilution → Seeding**

Very high cell density can alter growth, metabolism, morphology, and experimental responses. Very low cell density can reduce survival or proliferation for some cell types and may increase experimental variability.

## Practical 3 — Aseptic Technique

Aseptic technique refers to laboratory practices designed to prevent contamination of samples, cultures, equipment, or experiments by unwanted microorganisms.

### Sterile Pipette Tips and Media

Sterile pipette tips must be used when handling sterile biological samples or cultures. A contaminated tip can introduce microorganisms into a culture.

- Use sterile tips.
- Do not reuse tips.
- Use a fresh tip when necessary.
- Avoid touching the sterile part of the pipette tip.

Culture media must remain free of contamination. Bacteria, fungi, and mycoplasma can negatively affect cell growth and experimental results.

### Biosafety Cabinet

A biosafety cabinet (BSC) is a controlled-airflow environment used when handling biological materials. It helps reduce contamination and, depending on cabinet type, can also reduce exposure risks.

A biosafety cabinet and a laminar-flow clean bench are not the same. A BSC is designed for appropriate containment and protection of personnel, environment, and/or samples according to its design and the work being performed.

### Preventing Contamination

- Maintain proper hand hygiene.
- Use appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE).
- Use sterile consumables.
- Keep the work area clean.
- Follow proper pipetting techniques.
- Minimize unnecessary exposure.
- Dispose of waste properly.
- Clean equipment regularly.

### Common Cell Culture Contaminants

| Contaminant | Common indication or concern |
|---|---|
| Bacteria | Medium may become cloudy; pH or appearance may change |
| Fungi or yeast | Abnormal structures may be visible under the microscope |
| Mycoplasma | Often difficult to detect; growth, metabolism, and gene expression may be altered |

Mycoplasma is especially concerning because a culture can appear normal while contamination is present.

### Waste Disposal and Workflow

Segregate laboratory waste according to its category, such as sharps, biological waste, contaminated plastic, and chemical waste. Always follow the laboratory biosafety SOP.

**Prepare workspace → Use appropriate PPE → Use sterile materials → Minimize exposure → Handle culture carefully → Clean or disinfect according to SOP → Dispose of waste appropriately**

## Practical 4 — Pipetting

Pipetting is the accurate transfer of a specific volume of liquid using a pipette.

Common laboratory pipettes include:

- Micropipette
- Serological pipette
- Pasteur pipette

### Micropipette Ranges

| Pipette | Typical range |
|---|---|
| P10 | 0.5–10 µL |
| P20 | 2–20 µL |
| P200 | 20–200 µL |
| P1000 | 100–1000 µL |

Exact ranges can vary by manufacturer, so always check the pipette label.

### Basic Micropipette Principle

A micropipette uses an air-displacement mechanism to aspirate and dispense liquid.

- **First stop:** Aspirating or dispensing the measured volume
- **Second stop:** Blow-out of remaining liquid

### Basic Pipetting Steps

1. Select the appropriate pipette.
2. Set the required volume.
3. Attach a clean disposable tip.
4. Press the plunger to the first stop.
5. Immerse the tip slightly into the liquid.
6. Slowly release the plunger to aspirate.
7. Move to the receiving tube.
8. Press to the first stop to dispense.
9. Press to the second stop to expel remaining liquid.
10. Eject the tip.

### Important Precautions

- Never set a pipette outside its specified range.
- Use a new tip when contamination matters.
- Keep the pipette approximately vertical during aspiration.
- Avoid immersing the tip too deeply.
- Aspirate and dispense smoothly.
- Do not lay a pipette down with liquid in the tip.
- Change tips between samples when cross-contamination is possible.

Using the correct pipette range improves accuracy and precision and prevents damage to the pipette.

## Practical 5 — Serial Dilution

Serial dilution is a stepwise dilution technique in which a sample is diluted sequentially using a constant dilution factor.

It is commonly used in microbiology, cell biology, biochemistry, molecular biology, colony counting, cell counting, and assay preparation.

### Basic Dilution Equation

**C₁V₁ = C₂V₂**

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| C₁ | Initial concentration |
| V₁ | Volume of stock required |
| C₂ | Final concentration |
| V₂ | Final volume |

### Example

To prepare 10 mL of 1 mg/mL solution from a 10 mg/mL stock:

```text
10 × V₁ = 1 × 10
V₁ = 1 mL
```

Use 1 mL stock + 9 mL diluent = 10 mL final solution.

#### One-to-Ten Serial Dilution
A 1:10 dilution means 1 part sample + 9 parts diluent.

The dilution factor is 1/10 = 10⁻¹.

| Tube | Dilution |
| Tube 1 | 10⁻¹ |
|Tube 2 | 10⁻² |
|Tube 3 | 10⁻³ |


Serial dilution makes highly concentrated samples easier to measure and can bring a sample into a countable range.

## Practical 6 — Cell Viability

Cell viability is the proportion of living, healthy cells within a population. It is useful for evaluating cell culture quality, cell health, drug toxicity, treatment effects, experimental reliability, and suitability for downstream work.

**Viability (%) = Live cells / Total cells × 100**

### Trypan Blue Exclusion Assay

This is one of the simplest methods for determining cell viability. Live cells with an intact plasma membrane generally exclude Trypan blue, whereas cells with compromised membranes take up the dye and appear blue.

Example: 80 live cells and 20 dead cells gives **80/100 × 100 = 80% viability**.

### Other Viability Methods

| Method | What it measures |
|---|---|
| MTT assay | Cellular metabolic activity through reduction of MTT to colored formazan |
| Resazurin-based assays | Metabolic activity through conversion of resazurin to a fluorescent or colorimetric product |
| ATP-based assays | Estimated viable cell number based on cellular ATP levels |
| Flow cytometry | Viability using membrane-impermeable dyes and other markers |

## Practical 7 — Basic Cell Culture

Basic cell culture is the controlled growth and maintenance of cells outside the organism in suitable vessels, media, and environmental conditions. Good cell culture practice relies on aseptic technique, routine observation, accurate seeding, and careful record keeping.

### Core Requirements

| Requirement | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Appropriate culture medium | Provides nutrients and supports the cell type |
| Controlled incubator conditions | Maintains the temperature, humidity, and gas conditions required by the culture |
| Sterile vessels and consumables | Helps prevent contamination |
| Routine microscopic observation | Assesses morphology, confluency, and signs of contamination |
| Accurate cell counting and seeding | Supports consistent growth and reproducible experiments |

### Routine Culture Workflow

**Inspect culture → Prepare sterile workspace → Remove or replace medium as required → Detach cells when appropriate → Count and assess viability → Dilute and seed → Label and incubate → Document observations**

Monitor cultures for:

- Cell morphology
- Attachment
- Confluency
- Medium appearance
- Possible contamination

Follow the cell line-specific SOP for medium changes, passaging, incubator conditions, and cryopreservation.

### Confluency and Passaging

**Confluency** is the proportion of the culture surface covered by adherent cells. Cultures are commonly passaged before overgrowth affects physiology, morphology, or experimental behavior. The timing and target confluency depend on the cell type and laboratory SOP.

Passaging prevents overconfluence, maintains healthy growth conditions, and provides cells at an appropriate density for continued culture or experiments.

### Documentation
Accurate records of cell line identity, passage number, seeding density, media, treatments, observations, and contamination checks support traceability and reproducibility.
