# Genetics

## 1. Overview & Scope

**Genetics** is the branch of biology that studies genes, heredity, genetic variation, and how traits are passed from one generation to another.

It focuses on:

- Structure and function of genes
- Transmission of genetic information
- Inheritance patterns
- Genetic variation
- Relationship between genotype and phenotype

**Scope:**

Mendelian genetics, chromosome genetics, human genetics, population genetics, genetic disorders, genetic variation, genomics, and bioinformatics.

**Applications:**

- Disease genetics
- Genetic testing
- Personalized medicine
- Evolutionary studies
- Genomics
- Variant analysis
- Biotechnology research


---

# 2. Basic Genetic Terminology

## Gene

A **gene** is a unit of heredity that contains information responsible for producing a functional product.

Genes influence:

- Physical traits
- Biological functions
- Disease susceptibility


## Allele

An **allele** is an alternative form of a gene located at the same position (locus) on homologous chromosomes.

Example:
Gene for blood group
|
Alleles: IA, IB, i


## Locus

A **locus** is the specific location of a gene or genetic marker on a chromosome.


## Genotype

The genetic composition of an organism.

Example:
AA, Aa, aa


## Phenotype

The observable characteristics produced by genotype and environmental factors.

Example:

- Eye color
- Height
- Disease phenotype


## Homozygous vs Heterozygous

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Homozygous | Same alleles at a locus |
| Heterozygous | Different alleles at a locus |

# 3. Mendelian Genetics

**Gregor Mendel** established the basic principles of inheritance through experiments with pea plants.

Mendel's work explains how traits are transmitted between generations.


## Mendel's First Law: Law of Segregation

During gamete formation, two alleles separate so that each gamete receives one allele.


## Mendel's Second Law: Law of Independent Assortment

Genes located on different chromosomes are inherited independently.

# 4. Genetic Crosses

## Monohybrid Cross

A cross involving one genetic trait.

Example:
AA × aa
F1 generation:
All Aa


## Dihybrid Cross

A cross involving two different traits.

Example:
AaBb × AaBb


## Punnett Square

A diagram used to predict possible offspring genotypes and phenotypes.


---

# 5. Dominance Relationships

## Complete Dominance

One allele completely masks the effect of another allele.

Example:
AA > Aa > aa


## Incomplete Dominance

Heterozygote shows an intermediate phenotype.


## Codominance

Both alleles are expressed simultaneously.

Example:

- ABO blood group system


---

# 6. Chromosome Genetics

Chromosomes are organized DNA structures that carry genes.

Human cells contain:

- 46 chromosomes
- 23 chromosome pairs


## Homologous Chromosomes

A pair of chromosomes containing the same genes at the same loci.

One chromosome comes from:

- Mother
- Father


## Sex Chromosomes

Chromosomes involved in sex determination.

Humans:

- Female: XX
- Male: XY


## Autosomes

Non-sex chromosomes.

Humans:

- 22 pairs of autosomes
- 1 pair of sex chromosomes


---

# 7. Linkage and Recombination

## Genetic Linkage

Genes located close together on the same chromosome tend to be inherited together.


## Crossing Over

Exchange of genetic material between homologous chromosomes during meiosis.

Importance:

- Generates genetic diversity
- Creates new allele combinations


## Genetic Recombination

The formation of new genetic combinations through chromosome exchange.


---

# 8. Meiosis and Genetic Inheritance

Meiosis is the process that produces haploid gametes.

Importance:

- Maintains chromosome number
- Generates genetic variation


Key events:

- Chromosome pairing
- Crossing over
- Independent assortment
- Separation of homologous chromosomes

# 9. Patterns of Inheritance

## Autosomal Dominant Inheritance

Characteristics:

- One mutated allele is sufficient
- Usually appears in every generation

Example:

- Huntington disease


## Autosomal Recessive Inheritance

Characteristics:

- Two mutated alleles required
- Parents may be carriers

Example:

- Cystic fibrosis


## X-linked Inheritance

Genes located on the X chromosome.

Examples:

- Hemophilia
- Duchenne muscular dystrophy

# 11. Chromosomal Abnormalities

Chromosomal abnormalities occur due to changes in chromosome number or structure.

Presence of an extra chromosome.

Example:

- Down syndrome (Trisomy 21)


### Monosomy

Loss of one chromosome.

Example:

- Turner syndrome (45,X)


## Structural Abnormalities

### Deletion

Loss of chromosome segment.


### Duplication

Repeated chromosome segment.


### Inversion

Chromosome segment reversed.


### Translocation

Movement of chromosome segments between chromosomes.

# 14. Population Genetics

Population genetics studies genetic variation within populations.


## Allele Frequency

The proportion of a specific allele in a population.


## Genotype Frequency

The proportion of different genotypes in a population.


## Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium

A model describing allele and genotype frequencies in an ideal population.

Conditions:

- No mutation
- No migration
- Random mating
- Large population
- No selection


## Genetic Drift

Random changes in allele frequency, especially in small populations.


## Gene Flow

Movement of genetic information between populations.

# Required Practical - Genetics
## Practical 1 — ABO Blood Group Genetics

### Aim

To understand inheritance of the ABO blood group system.

### Principle

The ABO blood group is controlled by the **ABO gene**, which has three common alleles:

- **Iᴬ**
- **Iᴮ**
- **i**

Iᴬ and Iᴮ are **codominant**. The allele `i` is recessive.

### Genotype and Phenotype

| Blood group | Possible genotype |
|---|---|
| A | IᴬIᴬ or Iᴬi |
| B | IᴮIᴮ or Iᴮi |
| AB | IᴬIᴮ |
| O | ii |

### Key Concepts

- A person with blood group A may be homozygous or heterozygous.
- A person with blood group B may be homozygous or heterozygous.
- Blood group AB expresses both A and B antigens.
- Blood group O has neither A nor B antigen.

### Example Cross

If one parent has genotype `Iᴬi` and the other has genotype `Iᴮi`:

|  | Iᴮ | i |
|---|---|---|
| Iᴬ | IᴬIᴮ (AB) | Iᴬi (A) |
| i | Iᴮi (B) | ii (O) |

Possible offspring blood groups are:

- A
- B
- AB
- O

## Practical 2 — Pedigree Analysis

### Aim

To analyze inheritance patterns of a trait or genetic condition in a family.

### Principle

A pedigree is a family diagram that shows affected and unaffected individuals across generations.

### Common Symbols

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Square | Male |
| Circle | Female |
| Shaded symbol | Affected individual |
| Unshaded symbol | Unaffected individual |
| Horizontal line | Marriage or partnership |
| Vertical line | Offspring relationship |

### Common Inheritance Patterns

| Pattern | Typical features |
|---|---|
| Autosomal dominant | Usually appears in every generation; affected person commonly has an affected parent |
| Autosomal recessive | May skip generations; unaffected parents can have an affected child |
| X-linked recessive | More common in males; affected males often inherit the allele from carrier mothers |
| X-linked dominant | Affected father passes the trait to all daughters but not sons |
| Mitochondrial | Passed through the mother; affected mothers can transmit it to children |

### Pedigree Analysis Steps

1. Identify affected and unaffected individuals.
2. Check whether the trait appears in every generation.
3. Compare the number of affected males and females.
4. Determine whether father-to-son transmission occurs.
5. Consider whether unaffected parents have affected children.
6. Select the most likely inheritance pattern.

### Important Clues

- Father-to-son transmission suggests an autosomal trait rather than X-linked inheritance.
- An affected child born to unaffected parents suggests autosomal recessive inheritance.
- A trait present in every generation often suggests autosomal dominant inheritance.
- An affected father passing a trait to all daughters suggests X-linked dominant inheritance.

## Practical 3 — Karyotyping

### Aim

To examine chromosome number and large-scale chromosome structure.

### Principle

A karyotype is an organized display of chromosomes from a cell, usually arranged in pairs by size, banding pattern, and centromere position.

Humans usually have:

**46 chromosomes = 22 pairs of autosomes + 1 pair of sex chromosomes**

| Karyotype | Typical sex chromosome pattern |
|---|---|
| 46,XX | Female |
| 46,XY | Male |

### General Workflow

**Cell sample → Cell culture where required → Chromosome preparation → Staining → Microscopy → Chromosome arrangement → Karyotype interpretation**

### What Karyotyping Can Detect

- Extra chromosomes
- Missing chromosomes
- Large deletions
- Large duplications
- Large translocations
- Large inversions

### Limitations

Karyotyping may not detect:

- Small mutations
- Single-nucleotide variants
- Small insertions or deletions
- Many copy-number changes below cytogenetic resolution

## Practical 5 — Mendelian Inheritance and Punnett Square

### Aim

To predict possible genotypes and phenotypes of offspring.

### Principle

Mendelian inheritance describes how alleles are transmitted from parents to offspring.

Important terms:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Gene | DNA sequence associated with a trait |
| Allele | Alternative form of a gene |
| Genotype | Genetic makeup of an individual |
| Phenotype | Observable trait |
| Homozygous | Two identical alleles |
| Heterozygous | Two different alleles |
| Dominant allele | Expressed in a heterozygous individual |
| Recessive allele | Usually expressed only when two copies are present |

### Monohybrid Cross

Suppose:

- `A` = dominant allele
- `a` = recessive allele

Cross: `Aa × Aa`

|  | A | a |
|---|---|---|
| A | AA | Aa |
| a | Aa | aa |

Genotype ratio:

**1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa**

Phenotype ratio, if A is dominant:

**3 dominant : 1 recessive**

### Dihybrid Cross

A dihybrid cross studies two genes simultaneously.

Example:

`AaBb × AaBb`

Under independent assortment, the typical phenotype ratio is:

**9 : 3 : 3 : 1**

This ratio applies only when the genes assort independently and no other interaction affects the phenotype.

## Practical 6 — Sex-Linked Inheritance

### Aim

To understand inheritance of genes located on sex chromosomes.

### Principle

Sex-linked traits are caused by genes located on the X or Y chromosome.

### X-Linked Recessive Inheritance

X-linked recessive traits are more commonly expressed in males because males have only one X chromosome.

| Individual | Possible genotype |
|---|---|
| Unaffected female | XᴬXᴬ |
| Carrier female | XᴬXᵃ |
| Affected female | XᵃXᵃ |
| Unaffected male | XᴬY |
| Affected male | XᵃY |

### Important Features

- Affected males often inherit the affected X chromosome from carrier mothers.
- Father-to-son transmission does not occur in X-linked inheritance.
- An affected father passes his X chromosome to all daughters.
- Females may be carriers without showing the condition.

### Examples

- Hemophilia A
- Duchenne muscular dystrophy
- Red-green color vision deficiency

### Y-Linked Inheritance

Y-linked traits are passed from father to son because only males carry the Y chromosome.

## Practical 7 — Mutation Types

### Aim

To identify common types of genetic mutations and their potential effects.

### Principle

A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence.

### Small-Scale Mutations

| Mutation type | Description |
|---|---|
| Substitution | One nucleotide is replaced by another |
| Silent mutation | DNA sequence changes but amino acid remains unchanged |
| Missense mutation | DNA change results in a different amino acid |
| Nonsense mutation | DNA change creates a stop codon |
| Insertion | One or more nucleotides are added |
| Deletion | One or more nucleotides are removed |
| Frameshift mutation | Insertion or deletion changes the reading frame |

### Larger Genetic Changes

| Mutation type | Description |
|---|---|
| Copy-number variation | Gain or loss of a DNA segment |
| Deletion | Loss of a chromosome region |
| Duplication | Extra copy of a chromosome region |
| Inversion | Segment is reversed |
| Translocation | Segment moves to another chromosome |

### Possible Effects of Mutations

- No detectable effect
- Altered protein structure
- Reduced protein function
- Increased or abnormal protein activity
- Loss of gene expression
- Disease-associated phenotype

The effect depends on the affected gene, mutation location, and biological context.

## Practical 8 — Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium

### Aim

To calculate allele and genotype frequencies in a population.

### Principle

The Hardy-Weinberg equation describes expected genotype frequencies in an ideal population.

**p + q = 1**

Where:

- `p` = frequency of one allele
- `q` = frequency of the other allele

The genotype equation is:

**p² + 2pq + q² = 1**

Where:

| Expression | Meaning |
|---|---|
| p² | Frequency of homozygous dominant genotype |
| 2pq | Frequency of heterozygous genotype |
| q² | Frequency of homozygous recessive genotype |

### Example

If the frequency of the recessive genotype is:

**q² = 0.04**

Then:

**q = √0.04 = 0.2**

Since:

**p + q = 1**

**p = 1 − 0.2 = 0.8**

Therefore:

| Genotype | Frequency |
|---|---|
| p² = AA | 0.64 |
| 2pq = Aa | 0.32 |
| q² = aa | 0.04 |

### Assumptions

Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium assumes:

- Large population size
- Random mating
- No mutation
- No migration
- No natural selection

Real populations may deviate from these assumptions.

## Practical 9 — Genetic Markers and SNP Genotyping

### Aim

To understand genetic markers and the basic concept of SNP genotyping.

### Principle

A genetic marker is a DNA variation that can be identified and used to study inheritance, population variation, or disease association.

### Common Genetic Markers

| Marker | Description |
|---|---|
| SNP | Single-nucleotide polymorphism; variation at one DNA base |
| STR | Short tandem repeat; repeated short DNA sequence |
| RFLP | DNA variation detected by restriction enzyme pattern |
| Microsatellite | Repeated DNA motif with variable repeat number |
| CNV | Copy-number variation; gain or loss of DNA segments |

### SNP

A SNP is a variation at a single position in the DNA sequence.

Example:

```text
Individual 1: A
Individual 2: G
```
Possible genotypes in a diploid individual:
- AA
- AG
- GG

### Applications of SNP Genotyping
- Disease-association studies
- Population genetics
- Pharmacogenomics
- Ancestry analysis
- Genetic mapping
- Genome-wide association studies

### Common Genotyping Approaches
- PCR-based assays
- Allele-specific PCR
- Restriction-fragment analysis
- TaqMan assays
- DNA microarrays
- DNA sequencing

## Practical 10 — Genetic Disease Case Interpretation

### Aim

To interpret a genetic case using inheritance pattern, clinical information, pedigree data, and laboratory findings.

### General Approach

1. Collect the clinical history.
2. Review family history.
3. Construct or assess a pedigree.
4. Identify the likely inheritance pattern.
5. Consider appropriate laboratory tests.
6. Interpret results cautiously.
7. Recommend confirmation and genetic counselling when appropriate.

### Information to Consider

| Information | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Age of onset | Can suggest disease type or inheritance pattern |
| Family history | Helps identify a possible inherited pattern |
| Sex of affected individuals | Useful for considering sex-linked inheritance |
| Consanguinity | Can increase the likelihood of autosomal recessive conditions |
| Clinical features | Helps select relevant genetic tests |
| Laboratory results | May support or exclude a suspected diagnosis |

### Possible Genetic Test Types

| Test | General purpose |
|---|---|
| Karyotyping | Detects large chromosome abnormalities |
| PCR | Detects specific DNA targets or variants |
| Sanger sequencing | Confirms the sequence in a targeted DNA region |
| Gene panel | Examines multiple genes associated with a condition |
| Chromosomal microarray | Detects many copy-number changes |
| NGS | Examines many genes, exomes, or genomes depending on the test |

### Important Note

Genetic results should be interpreted with clinical context, family history, test limitations, and relevant professional guidance. A genetic variant may be pathogenic, benign, or of uncertain significance.
